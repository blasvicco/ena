Here are some thoughts and reactions from a close read of the manuscript.
Isolating physics indexing in the detection ablation
An important structural question arises in how the ablation study evaluates RQ1a, which asks whether preserving successful policies is sufficient on its own or whether the archive must be explicitly indexed by physical regime. In the current setup, toggling environment detection on and off alters both the total memory capacity and the learning dynamics, rather than isolating the indexing mechanism itself. Under the capacity rule in Section III-G, an archive without detection maintains a single pool of $K=5$ gladiators total, whereas an archive with detection maintains $K=5$ gladiators per discovered niche. This expansion changes how many population slots are protected from evolutionary replacement and alters eviction dynamics across phases. At the same time, although Section IV-C1 notes that environment detection acts only through the Hall of Fame, the mechanics in Sections III-E and III-H specify that a confirmed change also clears the return history $H$ and $R_{\text{min}}$, resets running input normalization statistics, and forces ten episodes of full-effort search ($\mathcal{P}=1$ while $|H|<10$).
Because these effects are bundled together, readers may wonder whether the reliability gains attributed to detection in Table VII stem from organizing the archive by physics, from multiplying the number of protected archive slots, or from the change-triggered recalibration of normalization and search effort. Introducing a control variant that holds total archive capacity and protected population membership constant—while keeping identical change-triggered resets and disabling only the niche index for storage and retrieval—would cleanly isolate the role of physics indexing. Without such a control, the answer to RQ1a would need to be framed as a contrast between bundled architectural configurations rather than direct evidence that indexing is the decisive factor.
Non-stationary Trust scores and archive ranking
A related mechanical tension appears in how specialist quality is scored and frozen inside the Hall of Fame. In Section III-E, Trust is defined relative to moving percentiles ($P_5(H)$ and $P_{95}(H)$) of the last 100 episode returns, and $H$ is wiped clean whenever a physics change is confirmed. Section III-G then specifies that a gladiator's Trust is frozen at the moment of induction and that an existing gladiator can only be displaced by a same-niche candidate with strictly higher Trust. Because the underlying scale of $\hat{r}(R)$ drifts as the population improves—and resets across regimes—frozen Trust values are not on a common metric.
This non-stationarity has two major implications for the paper's claims. First, it complicates the within-niche improvement mechanism described in Section III-G: an early specialist inducted when the recent return distribution was poor can receive a high relative Trust score that permanently blocks a later, objectively stronger specialist evaluated against a much stronger return history $H$. Second, it clouds the interpretation of the selection ablation in Section V-D. When the niche-match and best-Trust drivers select the gladiator with the highest frozen Trust, they are relying on a historical relative score that may misrank policies within the same niche. Consequently, the large advantage of Bayesian policy reuse (BPR) in Table X may partly reflect BPR's ability to overcome archive misranking via online return updates, rather than demonstrating the necessity of Bayesian inference over detector likelihoods and utilities. Retaining adaptive Trust for evolutionary selection within the active population while using a stationary, own-niche episodic return estimate for archive replacement and ranking—drawing on already collected acting episodes where possible—would resolve this discrepancy. Auditing archived Trust scores against common-seed, full-episode returns (following the validation pattern already established by the HalfCheetah Olympiad) would also clarify how much of the selection ablation effect comes from the scoring rule.
Truncated return extrapolation in the Plasticity mechanism
In the evaluation of Plasticity (Section III-E2 and Appendix A-B), candidate specialists are evaluated for $s = s_{\text{min}} + (s_{\text{max}} - s_{\text{min}})\mathcal{P}$ steps, and truncated returns are linearly rescaled to full-episode equivalents before updating Trust. When Plasticity is switched off ($\mathcal{P}=0$), candidates in CartPole are evaluated for only $s_{\text{min}}=10$ steps. In CartPole, where every step until failure yields a reward of 1, any candidate that merely avoids dropping the pole during the first 10 steps is credited with a rescaled return of 500—exceeding the 475-step solved threshold despite providing almost no evidence of full-episode balancing competence.
As a result, varying Plasticity changes not only the volume of search effort ($k$ and $s$) and mutation scale, but also the fundamental validity of the fitness signal fed into Trust. In the zero-Plasticity variants, evolutionary selection is guided by heavily censored 10-step survival estimates that saturate the return scale, offering a plausible explanation for the failure to acquire competent policies on Jupiter in Table VIII that is entirely separate from the smaller interaction budget. A similar question arises on HalfCheetah, where every candidate evaluation is capped at $s_{\text{max}}=200$ out of 1,000 steps and rescaled without verifying that 200-step performance preserves full-episode policy rankings. Decoupling the evaluation horizon from search effort, validating rank agreement across horizons, or adopting a fitness update that does not equate surviving a brief censored rollout with solving the full episode would strengthen the conclusions drawn from the Plasticity experiments.
Paired statistical inference and non-inferiority testing
There appears to be a mismatch between the blocked experimental design described in Section IV-B and the unpaired statistical procedures detailed in Section IV-D. Every replicate $k$ initializes the agents and environment streams from a shared seed, and in the selection ablation (Section V-D), the alternative drivers are evaluated on the exact same trained networks with byte-identical training logs. However, continuous contrasts are analyzed using unpaired Welch's $t$-tests and effect sizes standardized by between-group standard deviations. Because observations sharing a replicate seed—and especially those sharing an identical trained archive—are paired, confidence intervals, $p$-values, and the statistical power and sample-size calculations in Sections V-A and V-C ought to be computed from within-replicate differences. Depending on the sign and magnitude of the within-seed covariance, ignoring this pairing can either mask true precision or distort uncertainty estimates.
Separately, the decision rule for the Plasticity controller study in Section V-C states that an alternative replaces the linear ramp if its reliability is "not significantly lower" while using significantly fewer candidate steps. Treating a failure to reject the null hypothesis of equal reliability as evidence of equivalent performance is problematic, particularly when the family is powered at 80% only for large effects ($d \geq 0.81$). Reanalyzing the seed-matched contrasts with paired inference and formulating the controller trade-off around an explicit pre-specified non-inferiority margin would put the statistical conclusions on much firmer footing.
