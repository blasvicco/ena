\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Non-Stationary and Continual Reinforcement Learning}
A neural network trained by gradient descent on one task after another degrades on the earlier tasks. This failure was first described as catastrophic interference~\cite{mccloskey1989catastrophic}. Continual reinforcement learning studies agents that must keep learning in such conditions without losing what they already know~\cite{khetarpal2022towards}. Elastic Weight Consolidation~\cite{kirkpatrick2017overcoming} slows learning on the weights that mattered for earlier tasks. CLEAR~\cite{rolnick2019experience} replays past experience with behavioral cloning, and needs no task boundaries. When tasks are incompatible, a single network cannot serve them all. For such tasks, OWL~\cite{kessler2022same} gives each task its own policy head on a shared feature extractor, and chooses a head at test time with a multi-armed bandit. These three methods protect or partition the weights of one gradient-trained network. ENA, like the context-based methods below, preserves whole policies instead. An archived specialist is never trained again, so there is nothing to protect.

\subsection{Evolutionary Reinforcement Learning and Continual Neuroevolution}
Neuroevolution optimizes neural networks by mutation and selection over a population, instead of by gradients~\cite{stanley2019designing}. Evolution strategies~\cite{salimans2017evolution} and genetic algorithms~\cite{such2017deep} are competitive with gradient-based RL on standard benchmarks. They need many samples, however, and optimizing many parameters remains hard for them~\cite{khadka2018evolutionary,pourchot2019cemrl}. Hybrid methods use a population to diversify the experience of a gradient-trained learner~\cite{khadka2018evolutionary}. Nisioti et al.~\cite{nisioti2026continual} compared evolution strategies and genetic algorithms with PPO, continual RL methods and population-based RL, under changes of task and environment. Evolution strategies achieved the best balance between adapting and remembering. Genetic algorithms were the most plastic, but forgot more. The authors related this balance to how much the neighborhoods of good solutions in weight space overlap between consecutive tasks.

Earlier work on evolutionary algorithms for changing optimization problems developed remedies such as increasing diversity after a change, maintaining diversity throughout the run, and memory~\cite{jin2005evolutionary}. ENA uses these three (Section~\ref{sec:inspiration}). In the methods Nisioti et al. study, as in standard neuroevolution, the population evolves as a whole toward the current task. Changes are not detected, and earlier solutions are not stored and recalled. ENA adds both without gradients: it identifies the dynamics online, and it preserves specialists indexed by their physics.

\subsection{Quality-Diversity and Repertoire-Based Adaptation}
Quality-Diversity algorithms such as MAP-Elites~\cite{mouret2015illuminating} search for many diverse, high-performing solutions instead of a single optimum, and store them in an archive. Intelligent Trial and Error (ITE)~\cite{cully2015robots} computes such an archive offline, in simulation. With it, a damaged robot finds a working gait within minutes, by trying archived behaviors guided by a Bayesian model. TeLAPA~\cite{lillo2026beyond} brings the same idea to continual RL. For each task, it trains a base policy with PPO and fills an archive of behaviorally diverse policies around it with MAP-Elites. When a new or revisited task begins, short adaptation probes pick a starting point from all archives, so that competent alternatives remain available when a task is revisited. TeLAPA is evaluated on MiniGrid and on cyclic friction shifts in MuJoCo, HalfCheetah included, with the task boundaries given. ENA's Hall of Fame is also an archive of specialists, but it differs in two ways. It is built online, while the environment changes. It is organized by the physics the agent detects, not by a behavior descriptor.

\subsection{Change Detection and Policy Reuse}
Sequential change detection asks when a data stream has changed. Classic methods range from Page's CUSUM~\cite{page1954continuous} to Bayesian online changepoint detection~\cite{adams2007bayesian}. On CartPole, ENA uses the Predictive-Mixture CuSum~\cite{halme2026sequential}. It detects a change away from a known distribution without a model of the distribution after the change, by mixing predictors fitted on recent windows of data.

In RL, RL-CD~\cite{dasilva2006dealing} learned one model of the environment per \textit{context}, and recognized contexts by which model predicts best. ENA follows this idea. MBCD~\cite{alegre2021minimum} combined an ensemble of learned probabilistic dynamics models with a high-confidence change-point detector whose false-alarm rate is bounded. It created a new policy for each new context, and reused an earlier one when a context recurred. SWOKS~\cite{dick2024statistical} detects contexts with optimal-transport distances and Kolmogorov--Smirnov tests, and trains one policy per context. These systems are the closest to ENA in purpose, and they too preserve a dedicated policy per context. RL-CD is tabular, while MBCD and SWOKS train their models and policies by gradient descent.

ENA differs from these systems in how memory is populated and recalled, rather than in representation. Its specialists are individuals generated inherently by population search, rendering policy archiving free of dedicated training overhead. The search that produces them is scaled to failure. Recall weighs the detector's evidence together with the observed returns. Section~\ref{sec:scope} explains why these systems are not baselines here. To choose which stored policy to deploy, Bayesian Policy Reuse~\cite{rosman2016bayesian} keeps a belief over task types, updated from the returns it observes. ENA adapts it to an online, non-stationary setting, and adds the dynamics models' likelihoods to the evidence (Section~\ref{sec:selection}).

\subsection{Positioning}
Table~\ref{tab:capabilities} summarizes how related methods preserve and recall earlier behavior. OWL and TeLAPA are told the task boundaries during training, so they detect nothing online. ITE starts adapting when it senses a performance drop. TeLAPA is partly gradient-free: it trains with PPO, and fills its archives by mutation. ENA does not introduce any of these capabilities on its own. Change detection, recurrence recognition, whole-policy preservation, gradient-free learning and probing all have precedents. What ENA combines is threefold. Its archive is populated with candidate solutions generated naturally during evolutionary search (RQ1b). The archive is indexed online by physics the agent detects without labels (RQ1c). The search that feeds it scales its effort to failure (RQ2). No component computes a gradient. Unlike most methods in the table, ENA also assumes that it can reset the environment: to evaluate candidates and, on HalfCheetah, to play its chirp.
