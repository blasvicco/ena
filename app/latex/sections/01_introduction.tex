\section{Introduction}

Reinforcement learning (RL) agents are usually trained and deployed on the assumption that the environment does not change. When it does, for instance when a robot's physics shift between conditions, an agent with a single policy adapts by overwriting the parameters that encoded its previous behavior. This is \textit{catastrophic forgetting}~\cite{mccloskey1989catastrophic}. When the original conditions return, the agent has to learn them again.

Continual reinforcement learning studies agents that keep adapting without losing what they have already learned~\cite{khetarpal2022towards}. One family of methods protects or partitions the weights of a shared network, by regularization~\cite{kirkpatrick2017overcoming}, replay~\cite{rolnick2019experience} or task-specific heads~\cite{kessler2022same}. Another keeps a separate policy for each detected context and recalls it when that context returns~\cite{dasilva2006dealing,alegre2021minimum,dick2024statistical}. Neuroevolution offers a third route. It trains neural networks by mutation and selection over a population of candidates, instead of by gradient descent. Nisioti et al.~\cite{nisioti2026continual} showed that evolution strategies balance adapting to new tasks and remembering old ones well, while genetic algorithms adapt fastest but forget more. In these methods, however, the whole population evolves toward the current task. The population receives no signal that the environment has changed, nor indication of which earlier solution to restore when an earlier environment returns.

Evolutionary methods learn by trying many candidate solutions at once. Along the way, they produce many solutions that already work in the conditions they were tried in. The question addressed in this paper is whether a small set of skills can turn those solutions into a memory that mitigates forgetting. The skills are inspired by how organisms vary under stress and consolidate memories. The \textbf{main research question} is:
\begin{itemize}[\IEEEsetlabelwidth{\textbf{RQ1a}}]
\item[\textbf{RQ1}] When an agent encounters a sequence of non-stationary environments without explicit change notifications, does preserving effective policies indexed by detected physical dynamics—combined with failure-driven modulation of search effort—mitigate catastrophic forgetting?
\end{itemize}
Three narrower questions examine this memory mechanism in detail:
\begin{itemize}[\IEEEsetlabelwidth{\textbf{RQ1a}}]
\item[\textbf{RQ1a}] Is preserving successful policies sufficient on its own, or must the archive be explicitly indexed by the physical regime under which each policy was validated?
\item[\textbf{RQ1b}] Does policy archiving require additional environment interaction, or does it operate as an emergent byproduct of population evaluations?
\item[\textbf{RQ1c}] Can dynamic shifts and previously encountered regimes be autonomously detected and identified purely from unassisted agent experience?
\end{itemize}
Searching incurs computational cost, as candidate policies require evaluation in the environment. The \textbf{secondary research question} is therefore:
\begin{itemize}[\IEEEsetlabelwidth{\textbf{RQ1a}}]
\item[\textbf{RQ2}] Does dynamically concentrating search effort during performance failure achieve competitive retention and reliability while significantly reducing the computational cost incurred by uniform full-effort search?
\end{itemize}

To address these questions, the \textbf{Evolutionary Neuroplastic Agent (ENA)} is introduced. ENA evolves a population of small neural networks, termed \textit{specialists}. Each specialist is scored with a \textit{Trust} metric tracking recent return history. Search effort, termed \textit{Plasticity}, scales dynamically with the performance deficit of the active specialist. Validated specialists are archived in a \textit{Hall of Fame}. \textit{Environment detection} identifies dynamic transitions and recognizes previously encountered physical regimes strictly from unassisted agent observations. On CartPole, it evaluates predictive accuracy under transition models of each known regime. On HalfCheetah, it applies a structured diagnostic perturbation—a \textit{chirp}—and analyzes mechanical response features. \textit{Specialist selection} governs which archived specialist to deploy via Bayesian policy reuse~\cite{rosman2016bayesian}, integrating detector evidence with observed episodic returns. As a structural design constraint, no component of ENA computes gradients.

Two domains are studied. In CartPole~\cite{barto1983neuronlike}, the agent balances a pole on a moving cart. In HalfCheetah~\cite{todorov2012mujoco}, a simulated two-legged robot learns to run. In each domain, the agent trains on three environments in turn, which differ in gravity and other physical parameters (Section~\ref{sec:setup}). The evidence for the claim is a \textit{skill ablation}: the same agent, with each skill switched off in turn. Alongside it, standard single-policy learners were trained on the same sequences of environments, with the same number of environment steps: DQN~\cite{mnih2015human}, PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal} and SAC~\cite{haarnoja2018soft}. They show how much standard learners forget on these streams. They are a reference, not opponents. \textit{It is not claimed that ENA outperforms other continual-RL methods}, nor that gradient-free learning is what prevents forgetting. The question is only whether these skills mitigate it. Comparing ENA with continual-RL systems built for the same problem is left to future work (Section~\ref{sec:scope}).

The contributions, each tied to the question it answers, are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{A skill ablation} on CartPole, which switches each skill off in turn (RQ1, RQ1a, RQ1b, RQ2).
    \item \textbf{A selection ablation}, which compares Bayesian policy reuse with simpler ways of choosing an archived specialist (RQ1a).
    \item \textbf{A measurement of forgetting} in standard single-policy learners, on CartPole and HalfCheetah, as the reference for RQ1.
    \item \textbf{Gradient-free environment detection} for both domains, including the chirp for HalfCheetah, evaluated on a detection benchmark (RQ1c).
    \item \textbf{A study of what failure-driven search costs}: one run at full effort in every episode, and two alternative rules for turning failure into search effort (RQ2).
    \item \textbf{Reproducibility}: every result and setting reported here is generated by scripts, from the released code and experiment outputs.
\end{itemize}
