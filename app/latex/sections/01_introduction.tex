\section{Introduction}

Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) agents are usually trained and deployed under the assumption that the environment does not change. When it does, for instance when the physics a robot experiences shift between conditions, a gradient-trained policy adapts by overwriting the very weights that encoded its previous behavior. This is \textit{catastrophic forgetting}~\cite{mccloskey1989catastrophic}: once the original conditions return, the agent has to learn them again from scratch.

Continual reinforcement learning studies agents that keep adapting without losing what they have already learned. Nisioti et al.~\cite{nisioti2026continual} recently showed that neuroevolution is a competitive answer: evolution strategies achieve a good balance between adapting to new tasks and remembering old ones, while genetic algorithms are the most plastic but forget more. In both cases, however, the population evolves as a whole toward the current task. Nothing tells it that the environment has changed, and nothing tells it which earlier solution to bring back when a previous environment returns.

A different strategy is to keep a repertoire of behaviors and switch between them when conditions change. Quality-Diversity methods build such repertoires~\cite{mouret2015illuminating}, and Cully et al.~\cite{cully2015robots} showed that a damaged robot can recover within minutes by searching a repertoire of gaits computed in advance. Our question is whether an agent can build and use such a repertoire \textit{online}, from a non-stationary stream it is never told about, and without gradients.

We present the \textbf{Evolutionary Neuroplastic Agent (ENA)}, a gradient-free continual RL agent. ENA investigates whether an architectural synergy of gradient-free evolutionary search, failure-scaled plasticity, and physics-indexed specialist preservation can prevent catastrophic forgetting under non-stationary streams. ENA evolves a population of small neural-network specialists, scores each with a \textit{Trust} value, and scales its search effort, its \textit{Plasticity}, to how badly the current specialist is failing. Proven specialists are archived in a \textit{Hall of Fame}. Two further skills make that archive a usable memory: \textit{environment detection} notices when the physics change and recognizes physics it has seen before, using a domain-appropriate driver (linear-Gaussian dynamics models with a sequential change detector for discrete control; a wavelet-chirp spectral response for continuous control, where a learning population's changing behavior confounds transition-based detection); \textit{specialist selection} uses Bayesian policy reuse to decide which archived specialist to deploy, combining the detector's evidence with the returns it observes. We evaluate standard single-policy gradient-based baselines alongside ENA not to claim that neuroevolution replaces backpropagation for stationary function approximation, but to benchmark the severity of catastrophic forgetting when classical policy optimizers encounter physical shifts without architectural memory.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{ENA's architecture.} Environment detection with two domain-adaptive drivers: per-niche linear-Gaussian dynamics models and the Predictive-Mixture CuSum~\cite{halme2026sequential} for discrete control, and a fixed wavelet-train chirp whose spectral response separates physics for continuous control (Section~\ref{sec:chirp}), where a learning population's behavioral changes confound transition-model detection. Both drivers feed Bayesian policy reuse~\cite{rosman2016bayesian} adapted to an online, non-stationary setting, on top of a population governed by Trust, Plasticity and a Hall of Fame (Section~\ref{sec:detection}).
    \item \textbf{A skill ablation over \Res{ablation/n} replicates} on non-stationary CartPole. Each skill's contribution depends on the others. Plasticity is the largest single contribution. The Hall of Fame helps only together with environment detection, which adds \Res{ablation/ENA-HoF-vs-ENA-HoF+EnvDetection/test/rel/diff} points of test reliability without Plasticity and \Res{ablation/ENA-Plasticity+HoF-vs-ENA-Combined/test/rel/diff} with it. The combination reaches \Res{ablation/ENA-Combined/test/rel/mean}\%, against \Res{ablation/ENA-NoSkill/test/rel/mean}\% without any skill.
    \item \textbf{A comparison with DQN, PPO and SAC-Discrete at equal environment steps}, ENA's candidate evaluations included. The gradient-based agents forget an environment they had solved; ENA does not, and reaches a higher test reliability (\Res{challenge/ENA-Combined/test/rel/mean}\% against at most \Res{challenge/SAC-Discrete/test/rel/mean}\%, over \Res{challenge/n} replicates) in less wall-clock time. PPO, in turn, solves each new environment sooner, and SAC-Discrete scores slightly higher on the environments it does not forget.
    \item \textbf{The same comparison on continuous-control HalfCheetah}, against PPO and SAC over \Res{cheetah/n} replicates. ENA retains \Res{cheetah/ENA-Combined/retention/left/mean}\% of its performance on the environments it trained on first, against \Res{cheetah/PPO/retention/left/mean}\% for PPO and \Res{cheetah/SAC/retention/left/mean}\% for SAC; both differences exceed 65 percentage points and are highly significant. SAC, however, achieves substantially higher absolute returns within each environment it adapts to.
    \item \textbf{Negative results and reproducibility.} We report the alternative Plasticity controllers we tested against the one we use, including those the data could not separate from it, and every number in this paper is generated by scripts from the released experiment outputs.
\end{itemize}
