\section{Introduction}

Reinforcement learning (RL) agents are usually trained and deployed under the assumption that the environment does not change. When it does, for instance when the physics a robot experiences shift between conditions, an agent that keeps a single policy adapts by overwriting the very parameters that encoded its previous behavior. This is \textit{catastrophic forgetting}~\cite{mccloskey1989catastrophic}: once the original conditions return, the agent has to learn them again.

Continual reinforcement learning studies agents that keep adapting without losing what they have already learned~\cite{khetarpal2022towards}. One family of methods protects or partitions the weights of a shared network, by regularization~\cite{kirkpatrick2017overcoming}, replay~\cite{rolnick2019experience} or task-specific heads~\cite{kessler2022same}. Another keeps a separate policy per detected context and recalls it when that context returns~\cite{dasilva2006dealing,alegre2021minimum,dick2024statistical}. Neuroevolution, which trains neural networks by mutation and selection over a population of candidates instead of by gradient descent, offers a third route: Nisioti et al.~\cite{nisioti2026continual} showed that evolution strategies balance adapting to new tasks and remembering old ones well, while genetic algorithms adapt fastest but forget more. There, however, the whole population evolves toward the current task. Nothing tells it that the environment has changed, and nothing tells it which earlier solution to bring back when a previous environment returns.

Evolutionary methods learn by trying many candidate solutions at once, so along the way they produce many solutions that already work in the conditions they were tried in. We ask whether a small set of skills, inspired by how organisms vary under stress and consolidate memories, can turn those solutions into a memory that mitigates forgetting. Our \textbf{main research question} is:
\begin{itemize}[\IEEEsetlabelwidth{\textbf{RQ1a}}]
\item[\textbf{RQ1}] An agent meets a series of environments with different physics, one after another, and is never told when they change. If it keeps the solutions that worked, sorted by the physics they worked in, and searches harder when it is failing, does it forget the earlier environments less?
\end{itemize}
Three narrower questions look at this memory more closely:
\begin{itemize}[\IEEEsetlabelwidth{\textbf{RQ1a}}]
\item[\textbf{RQ1a}] Is keeping the solutions that worked enough, or must the agent also know which physics each one worked in?
\item[\textbf{RQ1b}] Does keeping them cost the agent extra interaction with the environment, or are they a free by-product of its learning?
\item[\textbf{RQ1c}] Can the agent tell from its own experience alone that the physics have changed, and recognize physics it has met before?
\end{itemize}
Searching has a cost: every candidate solution the agent tries must be played out in the environment. The \textbf{secondary research question} is therefore:
\begin{itemize}[\IEEEsetlabelwidth{\textbf{RQ1a}}]
\item[\textbf{RQ2}] Does searching harder only when the agent is failing reach results that searching little does not, at a fraction of the cost of always searching hard?
\end{itemize}

To answer them we present the \textbf{Evolutionary Neuroplastic Agent (ENA)}. ENA evolves a population of small neural networks, which we call \textit{specialists}, scores each with a \textit{Trust} value that tracks its recent returns, and scales its search effort, its \textit{Plasticity}, to how badly the specialist currently acting fails. Proven specialists are archived, frozen, in a \textit{Hall of Fame}. \textit{Environment detection} notices when the physics change and recognizes physics seen before, from the agent's own observations only: in one domain by how well a simple model of each known environment predicts the next observation, in the other by the body's response to a fixed probing sequence of actions, a \textit{chirp}. \textit{Specialist selection} uses Bayesian policy reuse~\cite{rosman2016bayesian} to decide which archived specialist to deploy, weighing the detector's evidence together with the returns it observes. As a design constraint, no component of ENA computes a gradient.

We study two domains: CartPole~\cite{barto1983neuronlike}, in which the agent balances a pole on a moving cart, and HalfCheetah~\cite{todorov2012mujoco}, a simulated two-legged robot that learns to run. In each, the agent trains on three environments in turn that differ in gravity and other physical parameters (Section~\ref{sec:setup}). The evidence for our claim is a \textit{skill ablation}: the same agent with each skill switched off in turn. Alongside it, we train standard single-policy learners (DQN~\cite{mnih2015human}, PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal} and SAC~\cite{haarnoja2018soft}) on the same sequences of environments at equal environment steps, to measure how severe forgetting is when nothing stores past solutions. They are a reference, not opponents: \textit{we do not claim that ENA outperforms other continual-RL methods}, nor that gradient-free learning is what prevents forgetting; we ask whether these skills mitigate it. Comparing ENA with continual-RL systems built for the same problem is left to future work (Section~\ref{sec:scope}).

Our contributions, each tied to the question it answers, are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{A skill ablation} on CartPole that switches each skill off in turn, and a \textbf{selection ablation} that compares Bayesian policy reuse with simpler ways of choosing an archived specialist (RQ1, RQ1a, RQ1b, RQ2).
    \item \textbf{A measurement of forgetting} in standard single-policy learners trained on the same sequences of environments, on CartPole and HalfCheetah, as the reference for RQ1.
    \item \textbf{Gradient-free environment detection} for both domains, including the chirp for HalfCheetah, evaluated on a detection benchmark (RQ1c).
    \item \textbf{A comparison of what failure-driven search costs} against searching at full effort in every episode, in a single replicate, and of alternative rules for turning failure into search effort (RQ2).
    \item \textbf{Reproducibility}: every result and setting reported here is generated by scripts from the released code and experiment outputs.
\end{itemize}
