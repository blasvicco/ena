\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We asked whether a small set of skills can turn the solutions of an evolving population into a memory that mitigates catastrophic forgetting. To answer it, we introduced the Evolutionary Neuroplastic Agent (ENA). On the tested CartPole sequences, the ablation supports it. Only the variants with failure-driven search learn the harder environments, and they use far more interaction with the environment. Among them, only the one whose archive is organized by the physics it detects keeps what it learned. Adding the archive also changes how the test policy is chosen, and the ablation does not separate the two. Recalling from the archive by Bayesian policy reuse is more reliable than following the detector alone. The memory is built online, without labels, by a detector suited to each domain. Whether scaling search to failure beats spreading the same effort evenly remains open. Single-policy learners trained on the same sequences forget much of what they learned. They also show where ENA falls behind: on CartPole, it adapts more slowly than PPO, and on HalfCheetah, it reaches lower returns than SAC. We did not test what causes these gaps.

The most direct next step is a hybrid of ENA with gradient-based learners, which can take several forms. In one form, learners such as SAC or PPO act as fast optimizers within a niche, to adapt sooner and reach higher returns. ENA's environment detection, Plasticity and physics-indexed Hall of Fame would then protect what each niche has learned across environment switches. In another form, gradient updates refine archived specialists before they are deployed. The population could also supply diverse experience to a gradient learner, as in evolutionary reinforcement learning~\cite{khadka2018evolutionary}. A hybrid is also the right agent to compare against continual-RL systems designed for the same problem: OWL~\cite{kessler2022same}, RL-CD~\cite{dasilva2006dealing}, MBCD~\cite{alegre2021minimum}, SWOKS~\cite{dick2024statistical} and TeLAPA~\cite{lillo2026beyond}.

The skills may also carry over to those methods. OWL and TeLAPA rely on being told where tasks begin and end. Their authors name, as future work, detecting task boundaries during training~\cite{kessler2022same} and handling weaker task boundaries~\cite{lillo2026beyond}. ITE starts adapting at a user-set performance threshold, and keeps no memory across conditions~\cite{cully2015robots}. ENA's environment detection supplies both the changes and the identity of a returning environment, from the agent's own observations. It could give each of these methods what it is now told or lacks. ENA's physics-indexed archive could also keep their adapted solutions for when a condition returns.

Several directions follow from the limitations. A skill ablation on HalfCheetah would test which skills ENA's retention there depends on. A control that spends the same search effort evenly, instead of by failure, would test the principle behind Plasticity. Repeated recurrence during training, randomized task orders and many more environments would test the memory beyond the evidence here. Nonlinear dynamics models, still fitted without gradients, could separate physics that the detector now merges. For systems that cannot be reset, the probe could be superimposed on the agent's own actions as a small dither. The contributions of the belief's two sources of evidence, the dynamics likelihoods and the test returns, remain to be separated. Specialist selection could also be learned, as a contextual bandit over the detector's evidence. Finally, the population's evaluations are independent and parallelize naturally on accelerators, which would allow larger populations and longer non-stationary streams.
